The navigation on this site is a small handheld device with a screen and
a click-wheel. You spin the wheel, the list of destinations slides past a
fixed highlight band, and you press the centre to go. It took an
embarrassing number of attempts to get there.

## Everything that came before

The Navigator went through a graveyard of ideas: an always-visible two-row
strip (fought the content for space), a bookmark-sheet (too
conventional), and a rotary dial where the menu items orbited on the
spinning surface (tanked the frame rate the moment you touched it).

The lesson from the rotary dial was the important one: **never put the
data on the gesture surface.**

## The split that worked

The Navigator separates two concerns that the rotary dial had fused:

- The **gesture surface** — the wheel. It does almost nothing; it just
  rotates. One element, one `transform: rotate`.
- The **data surface** — the screen. The destinations live here as plain,
  readable text, sliding under a fixed pointer (an Etch-A-Sketch model).

That separation is the whole trick. The thing your thumb touches is cheap
to animate, and the thing your eyes read never moves *with* your thumb.

## Two accumulators

The wheel keeps two independent rotation values:

- `wheelRotation` is **unbounded** — it drives the visible arc and spins
  forever, even past the end of the list.
- `listRotation` is **clamped and rubber-banded** — it drives the items,
  so when you hit the end the list tugs back while the wheel keeps going.

Each `pointermove` adds the *incremental* angle since the previous event,
never the absolute angle from drag-start. That one detail killed the
±180° flip-to-origin bug that plagued earlier builds.

## The performance contract

During any drag, only two elements may transform, and no style writes
happen synchronously inside `pointermove` — everything is batched into a
single `requestAnimationFrame`. The active item's label only updates when
you cross a detent, so text mutation is naturally rate-limited.

It's a lot of discipline for a navigation menu. But it's the most-touched
thing on the site, and it had to feel like a real device — not a demo.
